Training Loop — The Chef
"Cooks a dish, tastes it, adjusts the recipe, repeats."
  • model.train() — chef is in the kitchen, working
  • forward pass — cook the dish
  • loss — how far off from perfect
  • backward() — figure out what to adjust
  • optimizer.step() — actually change the recipe
  • weights change every batch
🍽️
Evaluate Function — The Food Critic
"Tastes every dish, takes notes, gives a final score. Touches nothing."
  • model.eval() — chef steps out, critic enters
  • torch.no_grad() — critic doesn't touch the recipe
  • forward pass — taste the dish
  • loss + accuracy — score in the notepad
  • weights never change
  • returns avg_loss, avg_acc

The Loss Accumulation Trick — why × data.size(0)?
Why multiply by batch size before accumulating?
Two schools each report an average exam score. School A has 30 students and scored 80. School B has 10 students and scored 90. You can't just average 80 and 90 — that ignores that School A is 3× bigger and should carry more weight. You need to weight by size first, then divide by total students.
Batch A — 32 images
batch size32
avg loss (from criterion)1.84
raw total loss32 × 1.84 = 58.88
Batch B — 28 images (last batch)
batch size28
avg loss (from criterion)1.61
raw total loss28 × 1.61 = 45.08
❌ Wrong — naive average
avg_loss = (1.84 + 1.61) ÷ 2 = 1.725
Treats both batches equally. Batch A had 32 images and should count more than Batch B's 28.
✓ Correct — weighted average
total_loss = (32 × 1.84) + (28 × 1.61) = 58.88 + 45.08 = 103.96
total_samples = 32 + 28 = 60
avg_loss = 103.96 ÷ 60 = 1.733  ← correct weighted average
Each image contributes equally regardless of which batch it landed in.
In the code:
total_loss += loss.item() * data.size(0) loss.item() is the batch average. Multiplying by data.size(0) converts it back to a raw total. Divide by total_samples at the end to get the true dataset-wide average.
